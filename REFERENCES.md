## Guidance and reporting

What the evidence-synthesis organisations ask of anyone who uses AI, and the reporting and appraisal tools the skills refer to.

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.70074"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Flemyng E, Noel-Storr A, Macura B, et al. Position statement on artificial intelligence (AI) use in evidence synthesis across Cochrane, the Campbell Collaboration, JBI and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence 2025. *Campbell Syst Rev*. 2025;21(4):e70074. Also published in *Environ Evid*. 2025;14:20.  
  **Why useful:** Joint position statement of four evidence-synthesis organisations. Reviewers stay responsible for their synthesis, AI is used with human oversight, and any AI that makes or suggests judgements is reported in full. The reference point for this workshop.  
  doi:[10.1002/cl2.70074](https://doi.org/10.1002/cl2.70074) · doi:[10.1186/s13750-025-00374-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13750-025-00374-5) · PMID [41230118](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41230118/)

- <a href="https://osf.io/fwaud/"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> RAISE: Responsible use of AI in evidence SynthEsis. Open Science Framework project.  
  **Why useful:** The recommendations the position statement supports: a framework for the responsible use of AI and automation across all roles in evidence synthesis, from reviewers to tool developers.  
  [osf.io/fwaud](https://osf.io/fwaud/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2026.08.007"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Fleurence RL, Qureshi R, Aggarwal R, et al. Generative artificial intelligence for systematic literature reviews: a good practices report of an ISPOR Special Task Force. *Value Health*. 2026.  
  **Why useful:** Good-practice report of an ISPOR task force, built on a rapid evidence assessment of 115 empirical studies (November 2022 to July 2025) and expert consensus. It supports AI for high-recall title/abstract screening and first-pass extraction inside human-in-the-loop workflows, and does not support autonomous or end-to-end review generation.  
  doi:[10.1016/j.jval.2026.08.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2026.08.007) · PMID [42763092](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42763092/)

- <a href="https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, current version. Chapter 4, Searching for and selecting studies; Chapter 5, Collecting data.  
  **Why useful:** The methods standard for the two stages practised in the exercise, screening and data extraction, written for human reviewers: the yardstick for any AI-assisted version.  
  [Chapter 4](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-04) · [Chapter 5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/current/chapter-05)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Page MJ, McKenzie JE, Bossuyt PM, et al. The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews. *BMJ*. 2021;372:n71.  
  **Why useful:** Reporting guideline with the 27-item checklist, the abstract checklist and the flow diagrams. The prisma-reporting-check skill applies it item by item, including the items on automation tools.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmj.n71](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71) · PMID [33782057](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33782057/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i4919"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Sterne JAC, Hernán MA, Reeves BC, et al. ROBINS-I: a tool for assessing risk of bias in non-randomised studies of interventions. *BMJ*. 2016;355:i4919.  
  **Why useful:** The risk-of-bias tool for non-randomised studies, used by the reference review of the exercise for its quasi-experimental studies. The risk-of-bias-assessment skill collects the evidence for each domain; humans judge.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmj.i4919](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i4919)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4898"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Sterne JAC, Savović J, Page MJ, et al. RoB 2: a revised tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials. *BMJ*. 2019;366:l4898.  
  **Why useful:** The risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, the other tool the risk-of-bias-assessment skill supports.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmj.l4898](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4898)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.39489.470347.AD"><img src="assets/badges/guidance.svg" height="18" alt="Guidance"></a> Guyatt GH, Oxman AD, Vist GE, et al. GRADE: an emerging consensus on rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations. *BMJ*. 2008;336(7650):924-6.  
  **Why useful:** Introduces the GRADE approach to rating the quality (certainty) of evidence. The grade-certainty skill drafts the reasons per outcome; the review team rates.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmj.39489.470347.AD](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.39489.470347.AD) · PMID [18436948](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18436948/)

## Evidence on AI in reviews

Reviews and primary studies that measured how AI performs on review tasks. Each line says what kind of study it is before what it found, using only what its abstract states.

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2026.06.012"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Fleurence RL, Qureshi R, Aggarwal R, et al. The use of generative artificial intelligence in systematic literature reviews: a rapid review of the literature. *Value Health*. 2026.  
  **Why useful:** Rapid evidence assessment of 115 studies (November 2022 to July 2025) of generative AI across eight review tasks. Evidence was strongest for title/abstract screening (51 studies) and data extraction (33); fully autonomous end-to-end review generation was unreliable.  
  doi:[10.1016/j.jval.2026.06.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2026.06.012) · PMID [42447981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42447981/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112221"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Laignelot F, Martin GL, Ossman M, et al. Large language models show promising performance for some systematic review tasks but call for cautious implementation: a systematic review. *J Clin Epidemiol*. 2026;194:112221.  
  **Why useful:** Systematic review of 63 studies (148 performance assessments, 77% on GPT models) comparing large language models with human reviewers. For title/abstract screening the median positive percent agreement was 0.92 and the negative 0.89; for risk-of-bias assessment the median accuracy was 0.62.  
  doi:[10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112221](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112221) · PMID [41831731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41831731/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2026.10118"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Adam GP, Davies M, Caputo E, et al. Artificial intelligence tools for evidence synthesis in the biomedical field: a living rapid review and evidence map. *Res Synth Methods*. 2026:1-25.  
  **Why useful:** Living rapid review of 134 studies of tools that automate or semi-automate review tasks. Large language models reached a median 88% recall in abstract screening and a median 63% accuracy in data extraction; the authors conclude that no existing tool can replace human expertise.  
  doi:[10.1017/rsm.2026.10118](https://doi.org/10.1017/rsm.2026.10118) · PMID [42847473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42847473/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Oami T, Okada Y, Nakada TA. Performance of a large language model in screening citations. *JAMA Netw Open*. 2024;7(7):e2420496.  
  **Why useful:** Prospective diagnostic study on 5 clinical questions of the Japanese sepsis guidelines, GPT-4 Turbo against conventional screening. Integrated sensitivity was 0.75, and 0.91 after post hoc prompt changes; screening 100 studies took 1.3 instead of 17.2 minutes.  
  doi:[10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496) · PMID [38976267](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38976267/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-301762"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Homiar A, Thomas J, Ostinelli EG, et al. Development and evaluation of prompts for a large language model to screen titles and abstracts in a living systematic review. *BMJ Ment Health*. 2025;28(1):e301762.  
  **Why useful:** Evaluation study in a living systematic review, GPT-4o against human decisions, with prompts developed on 1045 records and tested on 11 939 records and two updates. Sensitivity was 100% for the studies finally included, but 58-100% for the records humans had included at title/abstract stage.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmjment-2025-301762](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-301762) · PMID [40701625](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701625/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Gartlehner G, Kugley S, Crotty K, et al. Artificial intelligence-assisted data extraction with a large language model: a study within reviews. *Ann Intern Med*. 2025;178(12):1763-1771.  
  **Why useful:** Study within 6 ongoing systematic reviews (63 studies, 9341 data elements), extraction by Claude verified by a human against human-only extraction, with blinded adjudicators. Accuracy was 91.0% versus 89.0%, missed data items were the most frequent error in both, and the AI-assisted method saved a median of 41 minutes per study.  
  doi:[10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739](https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739) · PMID [41183336](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41183336/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofag401"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Ishikane M, Kataoka Y, Tsujimoto Y, et al. Efficacy of a large language model data extraction system in evidence reviews for emerging infectious diseases: a randomized crossover trial. *Open Forum Infect Dis*. 2026;13(7):ofag401.  
  **Why useful:** Randomised crossover trial with 5 experienced reviewers extracting data from mpox articles, structured summaries from OpenAI o3 against manual review of the PDFs. Mean time was 27.5 versus 34.5 minutes (the confidence interval of the difference includes zero), and accuracy was 100% in both arms.  
  doi:[10.1093/ofid/ofag401](https://doi.org/10.1093/ofid/ofag401) · PMID [42494833](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42494833/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2025-114044"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Fan S, Chen M, Doi SA, et al. Evaluating data extraction error by a large language model from randomised controlled trials: a large-scale empirical study. *BMJ Evid Based Med*. 2026;31(5):324-331.  
  **Why useful:** Empirical study of 664 randomised trials in children, Claude 3.5 Sonnet against a manually cross-checked data pool. 6.6% of 23 069 extracted cells were wrong, mostly data put in the wrong field or omitted; the authors call detailed checking of the output the primary consideration.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmjebm-2025-114044](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjebm-2025-114044) · PMID [42055792](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42055792/)

## Why it matters: workload and human factors

Why review teams want automation, and why a human who checks AI output is not automatically a safeguard.

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000326"><img src="assets/badges/context.svg" height="18" alt="Context"></a> Bastian H, Glasziou P, Chalmers I. Seventy-five trials and eleven systematic reviews a day: how will we ever keep up? *PLoS Med*. 2010;7(9):e1000326.  
  **Why useful:** Essay on the volume of the literature, with 75 trials and 11 systematic reviews published a day at the time, and the case for streamlining and innovation in review methods.  
  doi:[10.1371/journal.pmed.1000326](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000326) · PMID [20877712](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20877712/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.022"><img src="assets/badges/context.svg" height="18" alt="Context"></a> Hoffmann F, Allers K, Rombey T, et al. Nearly 80 systematic reviews were published each day: observational study on trends in epidemiology and reporting over the years 2000-2019. *J Clin Epidemiol*. 2021;138:1-11.  
  **Why useful:** Observational study of a random sample of 4000 PubMed records (1132 systematic reviews included). It estimates 29 073 systematic reviews indexed in 2019, about 80 a day, a more than 20-fold increase since 2000.  
  doi:[10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.022) · PMID [34091022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34091022/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012545"><img src="assets/badges/context.svg" height="18" alt="Context"></a> Borah R, Brown AW, Capers PL, Kaiser KA. Analysis of the time and workers needed to conduct systematic reviews of medical interventions using data from the PROSPERO registry. *BMJ Open*. 2017;7(2):e012545.  
  **Why useful:** Analysis of the 195 completed reviews registered in PROSPERO with a publication (July 2014). A review took on average 67.3 weeks from registration to publication, with a mean of 5 authors.  
  doi:[10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012545](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012545) · PMID [28242767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28242767/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.222176"><img src="assets/badges/human-factors.svg" height="18" alt="Human factors"></a> Dratsch T, Chen X, Rezazade Mehrizi M, et al. Automation bias in mammography: the impact of artificial intelligence BI-RADS suggestions on reader performance. *Radiology*. 2023;307(4):e222176.  
  **Why useful:** Prospective experiment with 27 radiologists reading 50 mammograms with a purported AI system that suggested a wrong category for 12 of 40 test cases. Readers at every experience level were prone to automation bias: inexperienced readers rated 79.7% correctly when the AI was right and 19.8% when it was wrong.  
  doi:[10.1148/radiol.222176](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.222176) · PMID [37129490](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37129490/)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2501001"><img src="assets/badges/human-factors.svg" height="18" alt="Human factors"></a> Qazi IA, Ali A, Khawaja AU, et al. Automation bias in large language model-assisted diagnostic reasoning among physicians trained in AI literacy: a randomized clinical trial. *NEJM AI*. 2026;3(5):AIoa2501001.  
  **Why useful:** Single-blind randomised trial with 44 physicians who had completed 20 hours of AI-literacy training, ChatGPT-4o suggestions with deliberate errors in 3 of 6 vignettes against error-free suggestions. Diagnostic accuracy was 73.3% versus 84.9%. For this workshop: AI literacy is necessary, not sufficient.  
  doi:[10.1056/AIoa2501001](https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2501001) · PMID [42932681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42932681/)

## Tools and further learning

Where to go next. Every tool here is a draft-maker: the human checks remain yours.

- <a href="https://asreview.nl/"><img src="assets/badges/tool.svg" height="18" alt="Tool"></a> ASReview LAB. Open-source software, ASReview project.  
  **Why useful:** In its own words, "an open-source machine learning tool for efficient, transparent, and interactive screening of large textual datasets", based on active learning. A different approach from prompting a chat assistant.  
  [asreview.nl](https://asreview.nl/) · [Source code](https://github.com/asreview/asreview)

- <a href="skills/EXTERNAL.md"><img src="assets/badges/tool.svg" height="18" alt="Tool"></a> External agent-skill collections for evidence synthesis, curated for this workshop: skills/EXTERNAL.md.  
  **Why useful:** Collections reviewed on 3 November 2026, with what each does well and what to watch for, and a security note to read before installing any skill.  
  [open](skills/EXTERNAL.md)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00638"><img src="assets/badges/evidence.svg" height="18" alt="Evidence"></a> Liu NF, Lin K, Hewitt J, et al. Lost in the middle: how language models use long contexts. *Trans Assoc Comput Linguist*. 2024;12:157-173.  
  **Why useful:** Experiments on multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval: performance was often highest when the relevant information was at the start or end of the input, and degraded in the middle. The reason the prompts put instructions first and keep one paper per conversation.  
  doi:[10.1162/tacl_a_00638](https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00638)

- <a href="https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15103882"><img src="assets/badges/exercise.svg" height="18" alt="Exercise"></a> Pruna ED, Davidescu L, Sorop-Florea M, et al. Multimodal hand hygiene interventions and clinical healthcare-associated infection outcomes in acute care hospitals: a systematic review of quasi-experimental studies. *J Clin Med*. 2026;15(10):3882.  
  **Why useful:** The reference review of the exercise (confirmed by the workshop team): a systematic review of quasi-experimental studies with 12 included studies, screening, extraction and risk of bias by two independent human reviewers. Details and limits in activity/data/README.md. Open it after the exercise, not before.  
  doi:[10.3390/jcm15103882](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15103882) · PMID [42194842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42194842/)
