Useful references

What the guidance asks, what studies have measured, why a human who checks is not automatically a safeguard, and where to go next. Every DOI and PMID was checked on PubMed and Crossref on 3 November 2026; each line introduces the study before what it found, using only what its abstract says.

Guidance and reporting

What the evidence-synthesis organisations ask of anyone who uses AI, and the reporting and appraisal tools the skills refer to.

  1. Guidance

    Flemyng E, Noel-Storr A, Macura B, et al. Position statement on artificial intelligence (AI) use in evidence synthesis across Cochrane, the Campbell Collaboration, JBI and the Collaboration for Environmental Evidence 2025. Campbell Syst Rev. 2025;21(4):e70074. Also published in Environ Evid. 2025;14:20.

    Joint position statement of four evidence-synthesis organisations. Reviewers stay responsible for their synthesis, AI is used with human oversight, and any AI that makes or suggests judgements is reported in full. The reference point for this workshop.

    doi:10.1002/cl2.70074 · doi:10.1186/s13750-025-00374-5 · PMID 41230118

  2. Guidance

    RAISE: Responsible use of AI in evidence SynthEsis. Open Science Framework project.

    The recommendations the position statement supports: a framework for the responsible use of AI and automation across all roles in evidence synthesis, from reviewers to tool developers.

    osf.io/fwaud

  3. Guidance

    Fleurence RL, Qureshi R, Aggarwal R, et al. Generative artificial intelligence for systematic literature reviews: a good practices report of an ISPOR Special Task Force. Value Health. 2026.

    Good-practice report of an ISPOR task force, built on a rapid evidence assessment of 115 empirical studies (November 2022 to July 2025) and expert consensus. It supports AI for high-recall title/abstract screening and first-pass extraction inside human-in-the-loop workflows, and does not support autonomous or end-to-end review generation.

    doi:10.1016/j.jval.2026.08.007 · PMID 42763092

  4. Guidance

    Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, current version. Chapter 4, Searching for and selecting studies; Chapter 5, Collecting data.

    The methods standard for the two stages practised in the exercise, screening and data extraction, written for human reviewers: the yardstick for any AI-assisted version.

    Chapter 4 · Chapter 5

  5. Guidance

    Page MJ, McKenzie JE, Bossuyt PM, et al. The PRISMA 2020 statement: an updated guideline for reporting systematic reviews. BMJ. 2021;372:n71.

    Reporting guideline with the 27-item checklist, the abstract checklist and the flow diagrams. The prisma-reporting-check skill applies it item by item, including the items on automation tools.

    doi:10.1136/bmj.n71 · PMID 33782057

  6. Guidance

    Sterne JAC, Hernán MA, Reeves BC, et al. ROBINS-I: a tool for assessing risk of bias in non-randomised studies of interventions. BMJ. 2016;355:i4919.

    The risk-of-bias tool for non-randomised studies, used by the reference review of the exercise for its quasi-experimental studies. The risk-of-bias-assessment skill collects the evidence for each domain; humans judge.

    doi:10.1136/bmj.i4919

  7. Guidance

    Sterne JAC, Savović J, Page MJ, et al. RoB 2: a revised tool for assessing risk of bias in randomised trials. BMJ. 2019;366:l4898.

    The risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials, the other tool the risk-of-bias-assessment skill supports.

    doi:10.1136/bmj.l4898

  8. Guidance

    Guyatt GH, Oxman AD, Vist GE, et al. GRADE: an emerging consensus on rating quality of evidence and strength of recommendations. BMJ. 2008;336(7650):924-6.

    Introduces the GRADE approach to rating the quality (certainty) of evidence. The grade-certainty skill drafts the reasons per outcome; the review team rates.

    doi:10.1136/bmj.39489.470347.AD · PMID 18436948

Evidence on AI in reviews

Reviews and primary studies that measured how AI performs on review tasks. Each line says what kind of study it is before what it found, using only what its abstract states.

  1. Evidence

    Fleurence RL, Qureshi R, Aggarwal R, et al. The use of generative artificial intelligence in systematic literature reviews: a rapid review of the literature. Value Health. 2026.

    Rapid evidence assessment of 115 studies (November 2022 to July 2025) of generative AI across eight review tasks. Evidence was strongest for title/abstract screening (51 studies) and data extraction (33); fully autonomous end-to-end review generation was unreliable.

    doi:10.1016/j.jval.2026.06.012 · PMID 42447981

  2. Evidence

    Laignelot F, Martin GL, Ossman M, et al. Large language models show promising performance for some systematic review tasks but call for cautious implementation: a systematic review. J Clin Epidemiol. 2026;194:112221.

    Systematic review of 63 studies (148 performance assessments, 77% on GPT models) comparing large language models with human reviewers. For title/abstract screening the median positive percent agreement was 0.92 and the negative 0.89; for risk-of-bias assessment the median accuracy was 0.62.

    doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2026.112221 · PMID 41831731

  3. Evidence

    Adam GP, Davies M, Caputo E, et al. Artificial intelligence tools for evidence synthesis in the biomedical field: a living rapid review and evidence map. Res Synth Methods. 2026:1-25.

    Living rapid review of 134 studies of tools that automate or semi-automate review tasks. Large language models reached a median 88% recall in abstract screening and a median 63% accuracy in data extraction; the authors conclude that no existing tool can replace human expertise.

    doi:10.1017/rsm.2026.10118 · PMID 42847473

  4. Evidence

    Oami T, Okada Y, Nakada TA. Performance of a large language model in screening citations. JAMA Netw Open. 2024;7(7):e2420496.

    Prospective diagnostic study on 5 clinical questions of the Japanese sepsis guidelines, GPT-4 Turbo against conventional screening. Integrated sensitivity was 0.75, and 0.91 after post hoc prompt changes; screening 100 studies took 1.3 instead of 17.2 minutes.

    doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.20496 · PMID 38976267

  5. Evidence

    Homiar A, Thomas J, Ostinelli EG, et al. Development and evaluation of prompts for a large language model to screen titles and abstracts in a living systematic review. BMJ Ment Health. 2025;28(1):e301762.

    Evaluation study in a living systematic review, GPT-4o against human decisions, with prompts developed on 1045 records and tested on 11 939 records and two updates. Sensitivity was 100% for the studies finally included, but 58-100% for the records humans had included at title/abstract stage.

    doi:10.1136/bmjment-2025-301762 · PMID 40701625

  6. Evidence

    Gartlehner G, Kugley S, Crotty K, et al. Artificial intelligence-assisted data extraction with a large language model: a study within reviews. Ann Intern Med. 2025;178(12):1763-1771.

    Study within 6 ongoing systematic reviews (63 studies, 9341 data elements), extraction by Claude verified by a human against human-only extraction, with blinded adjudicators. Accuracy was 91.0% versus 89.0%, missed data items were the most frequent error in both, and the AI-assisted method saved a median of 41 minutes per study.

    doi:10.7326/ANNALS-25-00739 · PMID 41183336

  7. Evidence

    Ishikane M, Kataoka Y, Tsujimoto Y, et al. Efficacy of a large language model data extraction system in evidence reviews for emerging infectious diseases: a randomized crossover trial. Open Forum Infect Dis. 2026;13(7):ofag401.

    Randomised crossover trial with 5 experienced reviewers extracting data from mpox articles, structured summaries from OpenAI o3 against manual review of the PDFs. Mean time was 27.5 versus 34.5 minutes (the confidence interval of the difference includes zero), and accuracy was 100% in both arms.

    doi:10.1093/ofid/ofag401 · PMID 42494833

  8. Evidence

    Fan S, Chen M, Doi SA, et al. Evaluating data extraction error by a large language model from randomised controlled trials: a large-scale empirical study. BMJ Evid Based Med. 2026;31(5):324-331.

    Empirical study of 664 randomised trials in children, Claude 3.5 Sonnet against a manually cross-checked data pool. 6.6% of 23 069 extracted cells were wrong, mostly data put in the wrong field or omitted; the authors call detailed checking of the output the primary consideration.

    doi:10.1136/bmjebm-2025-114044 · PMID 42055792

Why it matters: workload and human factors

Why review teams want automation, and why a human who checks AI output is not automatically a safeguard.

  1. Context

    Bastian H, Glasziou P, Chalmers I. Seventy-five trials and eleven systematic reviews a day: how will we ever keep up? PLoS Med. 2010;7(9):e1000326.

    Essay on the volume of the literature, with 75 trials and 11 systematic reviews published a day at the time, and the case for streamlining and innovation in review methods.

    doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1000326 · PMID 20877712

  2. Context

    Hoffmann F, Allers K, Rombey T, et al. Nearly 80 systematic reviews were published each day: observational study on trends in epidemiology and reporting over the years 2000-2019. J Clin Epidemiol. 2021;138:1-11.

    Observational study of a random sample of 4000 PubMed records (1132 systematic reviews included). It estimates 29 073 systematic reviews indexed in 2019, about 80 a day, a more than 20-fold increase since 2000.

    doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2021.05.022 · PMID 34091022

  3. Context

    Borah R, Brown AW, Capers PL, Kaiser KA. Analysis of the time and workers needed to conduct systematic reviews of medical interventions using data from the PROSPERO registry. BMJ Open. 2017;7(2):e012545.

    Analysis of the 195 completed reviews registered in PROSPERO with a publication (July 2014). A review took on average 67.3 weeks from registration to publication, with a mean of 5 authors.

    doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012545 · PMID 28242767

  4. Human factors

    Dratsch T, Chen X, Rezazade Mehrizi M, et al. Automation bias in mammography: the impact of artificial intelligence BI-RADS suggestions on reader performance. Radiology. 2023;307(4):e222176.

    Prospective experiment with 27 radiologists reading 50 mammograms with a purported AI system that suggested a wrong category for 12 of 40 test cases. Readers at every experience level were prone to automation bias: inexperienced readers rated 79.7% correctly when the AI was right and 19.8% when it was wrong.

    doi:10.1148/radiol.222176 · PMID 37129490

  5. Human factors

    Qazi IA, Ali A, Khawaja AU, et al. Automation bias in large language model-assisted diagnostic reasoning among physicians trained in AI literacy: a randomized clinical trial. NEJM AI. 2026;3(5):AIoa2501001.

    Single-blind randomised trial with 44 physicians who had completed 20 hours of AI-literacy training, ChatGPT-4o suggestions with deliberate errors in 3 of 6 vignettes against error-free suggestions. Diagnostic accuracy was 73.3% versus 84.9%. For this workshop: AI literacy is necessary, not sufficient.

    doi:10.1056/AIoa2501001 · PMID 42932681

Tools and further learning

Where to go next. Every tool here is a draft-maker: the human checks remain yours.

  1. Tool

    ASReview LAB. Open-source software, ASReview project.

    In its own words, "an open-source machine learning tool for efficient, transparent, and interactive screening of large textual datasets", based on active learning. A different approach from prompting a chat assistant.

    asreview.nl · Source code

  2. Tool

    External agent-skill collections for evidence synthesis, curated for this workshop: skills/EXTERNAL.md.

    Collections reviewed on 3 November 2026, with what each does well and what to watch for, and a security note to read before installing any skill.

    Open the list

  3. Evidence

    Liu NF, Lin K, Hewitt J, et al. Lost in the middle: how language models use long contexts. Trans Assoc Comput Linguist. 2024;12:157-173.

    Experiments on multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval: performance was often highest when the relevant information was at the start or end of the input, and degraded in the middle. The reason the prompts put instructions first and keep one paper per conversation.

    doi:10.1162/tacl_a_00638

  4. Exercise

    Pruna ED, Davidescu L, Sorop-Florea M, et al. Multimodal hand hygiene interventions and clinical healthcare-associated infection outcomes in acute care hospitals: a systematic review of quasi-experimental studies. J Clin Med. 2026;15(10):3882.

    The reference review of the exercise (provisional choice): a systematic review of quasi-experimental studies with 12 included studies, screening, extraction and risk of bias by two independent human reviewers. Details and limits in activity/data/README.md. Open it after the exercise, not before.

    doi:10.3390/jcm15103882 · PMID 42194842
